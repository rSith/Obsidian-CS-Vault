# 09.01 · Taylor Series and Discretisation

> [!info] [[09.00 Finite Difference Method|Lesson 9]] · Lecture 09 slides 6-3 to 6-7 · Priority 🟡 Medium (the basis of 2023 Q2(a) and 2026 Q3(a))

## 1. Discretisation (slide 6-3)
**Discretisation** means replacing a continuous problem (a function defined at every point) by a problem on a **finite set of points**.
- *First step in obtaining a numerical solution is to discretise the geometric domain → to define a **numerical grid**.*
- *Each **node** has one unknown and needs one algebraic equation, which is a relation between the variable value at that node and those at some of the neighbouring nodes.*
- *The approach is to replace each term of the PDE at the particular node by a **finite-difference approximation**.*
- *Numbers of equations and unknowns must be equal.*

**Grid (slide 6-5):** points spaced $\Delta x$ apart in $x$ and $\Delta y$ apart in $y$, labelled by indices $(i, j)$:
```text
 (i-1,j+1) ── (i,j+1) ── (i+1,j+1)
     │           │           │        ↕ Δy
 (i-1,j)  ──  (i,j)  ──  (i+1,j)
     │           │           │
 (i-1,j-1) ── (i,j-1) ── (i+1,j-1)
               ←Δx→
```
Notation: $f_i = f(x_i)$, $f_{i+1} = f(x_{i+1})$, $f_{i,j} = f(x_i, y_j)$.

*Numerical solutions can give answers at only discrete points in the domain, called **grid points**. If the PDEs are totally replaced by a system of algebraic equations which can be solved for the values of the flow-field variables at the discrete points only, the original PDEs have been discretised. This method of discretisation is called the method of finite differences.* (slide 6-5)

A **finite difference** is *a partial derivative replaced with a suitable algebraic difference quotient* (slide 6-6). Most finite-difference representations of derivatives are based on **Taylor series expansion**.

## 2. Taylor series (slides 6-4, 6-6, 6-7)
For a continuous function $f(x)$ with all derivatives defined at $x$, the value at $x + \Delta x$ can be estimated from a Taylor series expanded about $x$:
$$
f(x + \Delta x) = f(x) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x + \frac{1}{2!}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 + \frac{1}{3!}\frac{\partial^3f}{\partial x^3}(\Delta x)^3 + \dots + \frac{1}{n!}\frac{\partial^nf}{\partial x^n}(\Delta x)^n + \dots
$$
Replacing $\Delta x$ by $-\Delta x$:
$$
f(x - \Delta x) = f(x) - \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x + \frac{1}{2!}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}(\Delta x)^2 - \frac{1}{3!}\frac{\partial^3f}{\partial x^3}(\Delta x)^3 + \dots
$$

**Meaning of each term (slide 6-7 figure):**
| Term | Role |
|---|---|
| $f(x)$ | first guess (not very good) |
| $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\Delta x$ | adds the **slope** |
| $\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}\frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2}$ | adds the **curvature** |
| higher terms | more accuracy |

*In general, to obtain more accuracy, additional higher-order terms must be included.*

**Two variables** (slide 6-13, used for mixed derivatives):
$$
f(x+\Delta x, y+\Delta y) = f + \Delta x\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} + \Delta y\frac{\partial f}{\partial y} + \frac{(\Delta x)^2}{2!}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2} + \frac{(\Delta y)^2}{2!}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial y^2} + 2\frac{(\Delta x)(\Delta y)}{2!}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x\partial y} + O[(\Delta x)^3, (\Delta y)^3]
$$

## 3. Simple example
$f(x) = e^x$ at $x = 0$, $\Delta x = 0.1$ (true value $e^{0.1} = 1.105171$):

| Terms used | Approximation | Error |
|---|---|---|
| $f(0)$ | 1 | 0.105171 |
| + slope | 1.1 | 0.005171 |
| + curvature | 1.105 | 0.000171 |
| + cubic | 1.1051667 | 0.0000043 |

Each extra term reduces the error by roughly a factor of $\Delta x$. The neglected terms are the **truncation error** ([[09.02 First Derivative Differences and Truncation Error]]).

- Write both expansions $f(x \pm \Delta x)$ (with $1/2!$, $1/3!$) before any finite difference derivation.
