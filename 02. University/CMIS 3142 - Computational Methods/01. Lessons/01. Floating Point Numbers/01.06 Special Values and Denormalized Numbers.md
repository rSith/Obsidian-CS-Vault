# 01.06 · Special Values (±0, ±∞, NaN) and Denormalized Numbers

> [!info] [[01.00 Floating Point Numbers|Lesson 1]] · Lecture slides 24–27, 31 · Priority 🟡 Medium (asked in 3 papers, one part each)
> **Asked as:** "Describe the difference between normalized and denormalized numbers" (2024 Q1(b)) · "What is the special feature in the Denormalized number?" (2025 Q1(b)) · "Explain special values in IEEE 754 (±0, ±∞, NaN) and their use" (2026 Q1(d), 10 marks).

## 1. The problem these solve
The normalised rule $(-1)^S\times1.F\times2^{E-127}$ always has a hidden **1** in front. So **zero cannot be represented** by that rule (slide 24: *"hidden 1 prevents representation of 0"*). The solution is to **make exceptions**: two exponent patterns are reserved.
- $E = 00\ldots0$ (all zeros)
- $E = 11\ldots1$ (all ones)

## 2. The complete IEEE 754 encoding table (slide 27)

| Exponent E | Fraction F | Meaning | Value (single) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $00\ldots0$ | $00\ldots0$ | **Zero** (±0) | $\pm0$ |
| $00\ldots0$ | $\neq 0$ | **Denormalised** | $(-1)^S\times0.F\times2^{-126}$ |
| $00\ldots01$ to $11\ldots10$ | anything | **Normalised** | $(-1)^S\times1.F\times2^{E-127}$ |
| $11\ldots1$ | $00\ldots0$ | **Infinity** (±∞) | $\pm\infty$ |
| $11\ldots1$ | $\neq 0$ | **NaN** (Not a Number) | — |

(Double precision: same patterns with 11-bit E; denormalised value $(-1)^S\times0.F\times2^{-1022}$.)

## 3. Special values

### 3.1 Signed zero: +0 and −0
```text
+0 : 0 | 00000000 | 00000000000000000000000
-0 : 1 | 00000000 | 00000000000000000000000
```
- **Use:** zero must exist! The sign keeps the direction a tiny number came from when it underflowed (e.g. $-10^{-50}$ becomes $-0$), and gives correct signed infinities: $1/(+0) = +\infty$, $1/(-0) = -\infty$.
- $+0 == -0$ is **true** in comparisons.

### 3.2 Infinities: +∞ and −∞
```text
+∞ : 0 | 11111111 | 00000000000000000000000
-∞ : 1 | 11111111 | 00000000000000000000000
```
- **Use:** the result of **overflow** (a number too large to store) and of division of a non-zero number by zero. The calculation can continue instead of crashing. $\infty + 5 = \infty$, $1/\infty = 0$.
- Slide 35: when you see infinity, *"the chances are high that the reason is some programming error, or even worse, an error in your algorithm."*

### 3.3 NaN (Not a Number)
```text
NaN : x | 11111111 | any non-zero fraction
```
- **Use:** the result of an **undefined (invalid) operation**: $0/0$, $\infty-\infty$, $0\times\infty$, $\sqrt{-1}$. It signals "this result is meaningless" and **propagates**: any arithmetic with NaN gives NaN, so the error is visible at the end.
- NaN is the only value that is **not equal to itself** (`Double.isNaN(x)` is the safe test in Java).

> [!note] Beyond the slides
> The slides give the bit patterns. The example operations ($0/0$, $\sqrt{-1}$, $1/0$, NaN ≠ NaN) are standard IEEE 754 behaviour, needed to answer the "**and their use**" part of 2026 Q1(d).

## 4. Denormalised (subnormal) numbers

### 4.1 Definition (slide 26)
- **No hidden 1**: the leading bit is 0.
- **Allows numbers very close to 0.**
- $E = 00\ldots0$ → *"different interpretation applies"*.
- Denormalisation rule:
$$
(-1)^S\times0.F\times2^{-126}\ \text{(single)}, \qquad (-1)^S\times0.F\times2^{-1022}\ \text{(double)}
$$
- *"Note: zeroes follow this rule"* ($F = 0$ gives 0).

### 4.2 The special feature (2025 Q1(b))
The special feature is that **the hidden bit becomes 0 and the exponent is fixed at −126** (instead of $0 - 127 = -127$). This fills the **gap between 0 and the smallest normalised number $2^{-126}$** with evenly spaced values. Numbers that are too small to normalise lose precision gradually instead of jumping straight to zero. This is called **gradual underflow**.

```text
0 ── denormalised (evenly spaced, step 2^-149) ── 2^-126 ── normalised (spacing grows) ──►
```

### 4.3 Worked example
Smallest positive denormalised single:
```text
0 | 00000000 | 00000000000000000000001
```
$= 0.000\ldots01_2\times2^{-126} = 2^{-23}\times2^{-126} = 2^{-149}\approx1.4\times10^{-45}$

Largest denormalised single: $F$ = all 1s → $(1-2^{-23})\times2^{-126}\approx1.18\times10^{-38}$, just below the smallest normalised $2^{-126}$.

## 5. Normalised vs denormalised (2024 Q1(b))

| | Normalised | Denormalised |
|---|---|---|
| Exponent field E | $1 \le E \le 254$ (not all 0s or 1s) | $E = 0$ (all zeros), $F \neq 0$ |
| Leading (hidden) bit | **1** → $1.F$ | **0** → $0.F$ |
| Value (single) | $(-1)^S\times1.F\times2^{E-127}$ | $(-1)^S\times0.F\times2^{-126}$ |
| Value (double) | $(-1)^S\times1.F\times2^{E-1023}$ | $(-1)^S\times0.F\times2^{-1022}$ |
| Range (single, magnitude) | $2^{-126}$ to $(2-2^{-23})\times2^{127}$ | $2^{-149}$ to $(1-2^{-23})\times2^{-126}$ |
| Precision | full 24 bits | fewer significant bits (leading zeros in F) |
| Purpose | ordinary numbers | numbers very close to 0; gradual underflow |

## 6. Practical explanation
- Signal-processing and physics codes hit denormals when values decay towards zero; on some CPUs denormal arithmetic is much slower, so "flush-to-zero" modes exist.
- Spreadsheets and Java print `Infinity` and `NaN` rather than crashing, which is IEEE 754 at work.
