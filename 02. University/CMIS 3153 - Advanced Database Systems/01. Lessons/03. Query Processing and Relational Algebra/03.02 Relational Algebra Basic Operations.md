# 03.02 · Relational Algebra: The Basic Operations

> [!info] [[03.00 Query Processing and Relational Algebra|Lesson 3]] · Lecture L3 §3, §3.1 · Priority 🔴 High (needed for every RA question)

## 1. What is relational algebra?
**Relational algebra (RA)** is a **formal language** made of operations that take **one or two relations (tables) as input and produce a new relation as output**. Because the output is again a table, operations can be **chained (composed)** into bigger expressions, exactly like $(2 + 3) \times 4$ in arithmetic.

RA is the **theoretical basis of SQL**, and DBMSs translate SQL into RA before optimizing it ([[03.01 Stages of Query Processing]]).

> [!tip] Analogy: kitchen tools for tables
> A table is a tray of rows. **σ (select)** is a sieve that keeps only some rows. **π (project)** is scissors that cut away unwanted columns. **×** pairs every item on one tray with every item on another. **⋈ (join)** pairs only matching items. **∪, ∩, −** combine two trays like sets. **ρ** relabels a tray.

### Example tables used below
```text
Employees                                    Departments
EmpID | Name   | Salary | DeptID             DeptID | DeptName
------+--------+--------+-------             -------+-----------
  1   | Amal   | 60000  |  10                  10   | Sales
  2   | Binu   | 45000  |  20                  20   | Marketing
  3   | Chamal | 72000  |  10                  30   | IT
```

## 2. Select σ (filter rows)
**Syntax:** $\sigma_{\text{condition}}(R)$. Returns the rows of $R$ for which the condition (a **predicate**) is true. Conditions use $=, \ne, <, \le, >, \ge$ combined with $\wedge$ (AND), $\vee$ (OR), $\neg$ (NOT).

$$
\sigma_{Salary > 50000}(Employees)
$$
Result: rows of Amal (60000) and Chamal (72000), **all columns**. SQL equivalent: the **WHERE** clause.

Properties: the result has the **same columns** as $R$; number of rows ≤ rows of $R$; σ is **commutative** ($\sigma_a(\sigma_b(R)) = \sigma_b(\sigma_a(R))$) and a conjunction can be split into a **cascade** ($\sigma_{a \wedge b}(R) = \sigma_a(\sigma_b(R))$).

## 3. Project π (choose columns)
**Syntax:** $\pi_{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_n}(R)$. Keeps only the listed columns and **removes duplicate rows** (relations are sets).

$$
\pi_{Name,\ Salary}(Employees)
$$
SQL equivalent: the column list after **SELECT** (with DISTINCT, because SQL keeps duplicates by default).

> [!example] Duplicates disappear
> $\pi_{DeptID}(Employees)$ = {10, 20}: two rows, not three.

### Selection vs projection (lecture exercise)
| | Selection σ | Projection π |
|---|---|---|
| Works on | **rows** (horizontal) | **columns** (vertical) |
| Keeps | rows satisfying a condition | listed attributes |
| Duplicates | can't create any | removes them |
| Result degree (no. of columns) | same as input | ≤ input |
| SQL | WHERE | SELECT list |

## 4. Union ∪ (rows in either)
$R \cup S$: all rows in $R$ **or** $S$ (or both), **duplicates removed**. $R$ and $S$ must be **union-compatible**: same number of attributes with compatible domains (types), in the same order.
$$
\pi_{Name}(\sigma_{DeptID=10}(Employees)) \cup \pi_{Name}(\sigma_{DeptID=20}(Employees))
$$
→ names of employees in department 10 or 20. SQL: **UNION**.

## 5. Difference − (rows in the first but not the second)
$R - S$: rows of $R$ that are **not** in $S$. Union-compatible inputs. **Not commutative**: $R - S \ne S - R$.

$$
\pi_{DeptID}(Departments) - \pi_{DeptID}(Employees)
$$
→ {30}: departments with **no** employees. SQL: **EXCEPT** / `NOT IN` / `NOT EXISTS`. Difference is how RA expresses "**not**", "**never**", "**no**" ([[03.04 Writing Relational Algebra Queries]]).

## 6. Cartesian product × (all combinations)
$R \times S$: every row of $R$ combined with **every** row of $S$. Rows = $|R| \times |S|$; columns = columns of $R$ + columns of $S$.

`Employees × Departments` gives $3 \times 3 = 9$ rows, most of them meaningless (Amal paired with IT). Adding a selection on matching columns gives a join:
$$
\sigma_{Employees.DeptID = Departments.DeptID}(Employees \times Departments) = Employees \bowtie_{Employees.DeptID = Departments.DeptID} Departments
$$
This **(σ, ×) → ⋈** conversion is a key optimization rule ([[04.06 Heuristic Optimization and Transformation Rules]]): × produces huge intermediate tables.

## 7. Rename ρ
$\rho_{S}(R)$ renames relation $R$ to $S$; $\rho_{S(B_1, \dots, B_n)}(R)$ also renames its attributes. Needed when the **same table is used twice** (self-join) or to avoid name clashes.
$$
\rho_{Staff}(Employees)
$$
SQL: table aliases (`FROM Employees AS E1, Employees AS E2`) and column aliases (`AS`).

## 8. Fundamental vs derived operations
The six operations **σ, π, ∪, −, ×, ρ** are the **fundamental** set: every other operation (∩, joins, division) can be written using them:
$$
R \cap S = R - (R - S) \qquad R \bowtie_\theta S = \sigma_\theta(R \times S)
$$

## 9. Operator precedence and associativity (2020/21 Q7(b))
> [!warning] Not stated in the lecture notes
> The lecture never states an operator precedence for RA. The usual convention (textbooks): **unary operators (σ, π, ρ) bind tightest**, then **× and ⋈**, then **∩**, then **∪ and −**; operators of equal precedence are evaluated **left to right**. In practice **always use parentheses**. Associativity (lecture transformation rule 9): **×, ⋈, ∪, ∩ are associative** ($(R \theta S)\theta T = R \theta (S \theta T)$); **− is not** ($(R - S) - T \ne R - (S - T)$). Unary operators nest right-to-left: $\pi_A\sigma_c R = \pi_A(\sigma_c(R))$. This information could not be verified from the available course materials. See [[PP 03.4 - Relational Algebra Theory Questions]].
