# 05.04 · Data Fragmentation, Replication and Allocation

> [!info] [[05.00 Distributed Database Systems|Lesson 5]] · Lecture L5 §5.1–§5.3 · Priority 🔴 High
> **Asked as:**
> - "Describe the data distribution approaches Data Fragmentation and Replication, highlighting their benefits and drawbacks compared to a centralised database." (2021/22 Q3(d))
> - "Discuss appropriate data fragmentation and replication strategies for storing the customer orders database across the company's three locations, with justification." (2023/24 Q6(b))
> - "Suggest appropriate data distribution strategies (fragmentation, replication, or allocation) for this system (New York, London, Singapore)." (2023/24 Q7(d)(i))

## 1. The three design decisions
1. **Fragmentation:** break relations into smaller logical units (**fragments**).
2. **Replication:** store some fragments at **more than one site**.
3. **Allocation (data distribution):** decide **which site(s)** each fragment or copy goes to.
The results are recorded in a **global directory** used by the DDBMS.

> [!tip] Analogy: a supermarket chain's stock records
> **Horizontal fragmentation** = each branch keeps the records of *its own* stock (rows split by branch). **Vertical fragmentation** = the HR office keeps employees' salary details while the canteen keeps their meal preferences (columns split by use). **Replication** = every branch keeps a copy of the price list. **Allocation** = deciding which branch's computer holds which file.

## 2. Horizontal fragmentation (lecture §5.2.1.1)
A **horizontal fragment** is a **subset of the rows** of a relation, chosen by a condition on one or more attributes:
$$
R_i = \sigma_{C_i}(R)
$$
Example: $\sigma_{Dno = 5}(EMPLOYEE)$ = all employees of department 5, stored at department 5's site.
- **Complete:** every tuple satisfies some $C_i$ ($C_1 \vee C_2 \vee \dots \vee C_n$ covers $R$).
- **Disjoint:** no tuple satisfies two conditions.
- **Reconstruction:** $R = R_1 \cup R_2 \cup \dots \cup R_n$ (**UNION**).
- **Derived horizontal fragmentation:** fragment a *secondary* relation the same way as a *primary* one it references through a foreign key (e.g. split EMPLOYEE and PROJECT by the department they belong to, matching DEPARTMENT's fragments), so related data lands together.

## 3. Vertical fragmentation (lecture §5.2.1.2)
A **vertical fragment** keeps only **some columns**:
$$
R_i = \pi_{L_i}(R)
$$
Example: EMPLOYEE → personal fragment {**Ssn**, Name, Bdate, Address, Sex} and work fragment {**Ssn**, Salary, Super_ssn, Dno}.
- **Every vertical fragment must include the primary key** (or a candidate key); otherwise the original tuples cannot be put back together (the lecture's first, "not quite proper" split had no common attribute).
- **Complete vertical fragmentation:** $L_1 \cup L_2 \cup \dots \cup L_n = ATTRS(R)$ and $L_i \cap L_j = PK(R)$ for $i \ne j$.
- **Reconstruction:** **OUTER UNION** (or a join on the key) of the fragments.

## 4. Mixed (hybrid) fragmentation (lecture §5.2.1.3)
Apply both: e.g. split EMPLOYEE by department (rows), then split each piece into personal/work columns. Reconstruct with UNION and OUTER UNION/OUTER JOIN in the right order. In general any fragment is
$$
\pi_L(\sigma_C(R))
$$
| C | L | Fragment type |
|---|---|---|
| TRUE | ≠ ATTRS(R) | vertical |
| ≠ TRUE | = ATTRS(R) | horizontal |
| ≠ TRUE | ≠ ATTRS(R) | mixed |
| TRUE | = ATTRS(R) | the whole relation |

## 5. Fragmentation schema and allocation schema (lecture §5.2.1.4)
- **Fragmentation schema:** the definition of all fragments; covers all attributes and tuples; the database can be **reconstructed** by UNION and OUTER UNION/OUTER JOIN. Ideally fragments are **disjoint** (apart from repeated keys in vertical fragments).
- **Allocation schema:** a mapping **fragment → site(s)**. A fragment stored at more than one site is **replicated**. (A description of the copies is a **replication schema**.)

## 6. Replication and allocation (lecture §5.3)
| Strategy | Description | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Full replication** | the **whole database at every site** | **maximum availability** (works while any one site is up); **fast retrieval**: global queries answered locally | **updates very slow** (every copy must be updated); **concurrency control and recovery** much more expensive; storage |
| **No replication** (non-redundant allocation) | each fragment at **exactly one site**; fragments disjoint (except keys of vertical fragments) | cheap updates; simple consistency | a site failure makes its data unavailable; remote reads |
| **Partial replication** | some fragments replicated, others not; number of copies from 1 to all sites | balance chosen per fragment | design complexity |

**Choosing (lecture guidelines)**
- High availability needed + transactions at any site + mostly **read-only** → **full replication**.
- Data used mostly at **one site** → allocate it **only there**.
- Data accessed at **several sites** → **replicate** at those sites.
- **Many updates** → **limit replication**.
- Optimal allocation is a **complex optimization problem**.

## 7. Benefits and drawbacks vs a centralized database (2021/22 Q3(d))
| | Benefits | Drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| **Fragmentation** | data stored **where it is used** (locality) → less network traffic, faster local queries; **parallel** execution over fragments; smaller relations to scan; local security (sensitive columns kept at one site); local autonomy | **global queries** must combine fragments (joins/unions across sites → communication cost); harder **integrity checking** across fragments; complex design (choosing fragments); vertical fragments need joins to rebuild rows |
| **Replication** | **higher availability and reliability** (no single point of failure); **faster reads** from a nearby copy; load sharing; disaster recovery | **update propagation** to all copies (slower writes); **consistency** management, concurrency control and recovery harder; more storage; risk of stale/conflicting copies (CAP) |
| **Centralized DB** (for contrast) | simple, consistent, easy integrity, no communication cost inside | single point of failure, remote users suffer latency, limited scalability |

## 8. Scenario: customer orders in Colombo, London, Singapore (2023/24 Q6(b), Q7(d)(i))
**Fragmentation**
- **Orders / OrderLines / local Customers:** **horizontal fragmentation by branch** (`σ_{branch='Colombo'}(Orders)`, …). Each branch mainly creates and queries its own orders → local, fast, and still available if the WAN fails. Use **derived horizontal fragmentation** for OrderLines (follow their Order) so an order and its lines live together.
- **Customer table:** vertical fragmentation can separate **sensitive payment/personal columns** (kept at the home branch for privacy/data-protection law) from **general profile columns**, each fragment keeping `Customer_ID`.

**Replication**
- **Product catalogue, prices, branch list, exchange rates:** **replicated at all three sites** (read-heavy, rarely updated) → local reads, high availability.
- **Order fragments:** keep the master at the home branch, plus **one asynchronous replica** at another site (e.g. Colombo ↔ Singapore) or a central reporting site for **disaster recovery** and global reports. Avoid full replication of orders: constant inserts would need updating at every site.

**Allocation**
- Each branch's order fragment → **its own site**; replica → partner site/head office; global catalogue → all sites; analytics → a data warehouse ([[07.00 Data Warehousing and OLAP]]).

**Justification:** maximizes **locality** (most transactions single-site), **availability** (local operation during partitions; replicas for failures), and keeps **update cost** low by not replicating write-heavy data everywhere; global reports use union of fragments (fragmentation transparency).
