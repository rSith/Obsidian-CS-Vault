> [!tip] Two formulas do all the work
> $$\text{Nyquist (noiseless): } R_{max} = 2B\log_2 V\qquad \text{Shannon (noisy): } C = B\log_2(1+S/N)\qquad S/N = 10^{\text{dB}/10}$$

## A. Nyquist / Shannon calculations

### 2018/19 Q3(d)
> **Question:** Television channels are 6 MHz wide. iii. How many bits/sec can be sent if six-level digital signals are used? Assume a noiseless channel. iv. If a channel has a signal-to-noise ratio of 30 dB, what is the maximum achievable data rate?

**Solution (iii)** Given B = 6 MHz = 6×10⁶ Hz, V = 6 → Nyquist
$$R = 2 \times 6\times10^6 \times \log_2 6 = 12\times10^6 \times 2.585 = 31.0\times10^6\ \text{bps}$$
**Answer ≈ 31 Mbps.**

**Solution (iv)** 30 dB → $S/N = 10^{30/10} = 1000$ → Shannon
$$C = 6\times10^6 \times \log_2(1+1000) = 6\times10^6 \times 9.967 = 59.8\times10^6\ \text{bps}$$
**Answer ≈ 59.8 Mbps.**

### 2018/19 Q4(a)
> **Question:** A cable company uses one of the cable TV channels with a bandwidth of 4 MHz to provide digital communication to each residence. What is the maximum available data rate of each resident if the company uses the following techniques for transmission? I. QAM - 16 II. QAM - 64

**Solution** B = 4×10⁶ Hz.
- QAM-16: V = 16, $\log_2 16 = 4$ → $R = 2\times4\times10^6\times4 = $ **32 Mbps**
- QAM-64: V = 64, $\log_2 64 = 6$ → $R = 2\times4\times10^6\times6 = $ **48 Mbps**
(Assumes a noiseless channel and one channel per residence.)

### 2019/20 Q4(d)
> **Question:** A noiseless 8-kHz channel uses Quadrature Amplitude Modulation-16 (QAM-16) for data transmission. i. What is the maximum data rate? ii. How does the maximum data rate change if the channel is noisy, with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 30 dB?

**(i)** $R = 2 \times 8000 \times \log_2 16 = 16000 \times 4 = $ **64 kbps**
**(ii)** $S/N = 1000$ → $C = 8000 \times \log_2 1001 = 8000 \times 9.967 \approx$ **79.7 kbps**.
**Interpretation:** the Shannon limit (79.7 kbps) is higher than QAM-16's Nyquist limit (64 kbps), so with QAM-16 the channel can still reach **64 kbps**; no signalling scheme could exceed ≈ 79.7 kbps on this noisy channel.

### 2022/23 Q3(d)
> **Question:** Consider a noiseless 3-kHz channel that transmit binary signals. i. Show that the above channel cannot exceed a data rate of 6000 bps. ii. If the above channel is subjected to a noise of 40dB, what will be the highest possible data rate?

**(i)** Binary → V = 2. Nyquist gives the **maximum** rate for a noiseless channel: $R = 2 \times 3000 \times \log_2 2 = 6000$ bps. Since this is an upper bound, the channel **cannot exceed 6000 bps**. (Intuition: a 3 kHz channel can carry at most $2B = 6000$ independent samples/s, each holding 1 bit.)
**(ii)** 40 dB → $S/N = 10^4 = 10{,}000$ → $C = 3000 \times \log_2(10001) = 3000 \times 13.288 \approx$ **39.9 kbps**.
**Interpretation:** 39.9 kbps is the highest possible rate with any (multi-level) signalling; with **binary** signals the channel is still limited to **6 kbps**.

### 2023/24 Q4(c)
> **Question:** A noiseless 4-kHz channel uses Quadrature Amplitude Modulation-64 (QAM-64) for data transmission. i. What is the maximum data rate? ii. How does the maximum data rate change if the channel is noisy, with a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of 20 dB?

**(i)** $R = 2 \times 4000 \times \log_2 64 = 8000 \times 6 = $ **48 kbps**
**(ii)** 20 dB → $S/N = 100$ → $C = 4000 \times \log_2 101 = 4000 \times 6.658 \approx$ **26.6 kbps**.
**Interpretation:** noise **reduces** the maximum from 48 kbps to about **26.6 kbps** (the Shannon limit is now the binding limit; QAM-64 cannot be used at full speed).

### Exam approach for every calculation
**Given → formula → substitution (with units in Hz) → calculation → answer with units → one-line interpretation.** Show the dB → ratio conversion explicitly.

## B. The dog and the tapes

> **2021/22 Q4(c):** Imagine that you have trained your pet dog to carry a box of three 8-mm tapes. These tapes each contain 10 gigabytes. The dog can travel to your side, wherever you may be, at 18 km/hour. For what range of distances does the dog have a higher data rate than a transmission line whose data rate (excluding overhead) is 150Mbps? How does your answer change if, i. Dog's speed is doubled; ii. each tape capacity is doubled; iii. the data rate of the transmission line is doubled.

> [!note] Not worked in the slides
> This is a Tanenbaum Chapter 2 problem; the lecture's link is **magnetic media** (carrying tapes physically).

**Given:** data = 3 × 10 GB = 30 GB = $30\times10^9\times8 = 2.4\times10^{11}$ bits; dog speed 18 km/h; line 150 Mbps.
**Line time:** $t = \dfrac{2.4\times10^{11}}{1.5\times10^{8}} = 1600$ s.
**Dog time** for d km: $\dfrac{d}{18}$ h $= 200d$ s.
**Dog wins when** $200d < 1600$ → $d < $ **8 km**.

| Change | Break-even distance |
|---|---|
| (i) Dog's speed doubled → 36 km/h → $100d < 1600$ | **d < 16 km** |
| (ii) Tape capacity doubled → line needs 3200 s | **d < 16 km** |
| (iii) Line rate doubled → 300 Mbps → 800 s | **d < 4 km** |

*(Assumption: 1 GB = 10⁹ bytes. With 1 GB = 2³⁰ bytes the answer is ≈ 8.6 km.)* Point to make: the dog has enormous **bandwidth** but large **delay**; doubling capacity or speed doubles the range in which it wins.

## C. Baseband vs passband

> **2019/20 Q4(b):** What is Baseband and Passband transmissions? Describe with an example.
> **2021/22 Q4(b), 2022/23 Q4(b):** Explain what is Baseband and Passband transmission and list down the techniques used in each transmission type.

### Expected answer
Digital modulation converts bits into analog signals. **Baseband transmission:** the signal occupies frequencies **from zero up to a maximum** depending on the signalling rate; bits are converted **directly** into voltage levels (or light on/off); common for **wires**. Techniques: **NRZ** (+V = 1, −V = 0), **NRZI** (1 = transition, 0 = no transition; used by **USB**). Example: Ethernet over twisted pair.
**Passband transmission:** bits are sent by **regulating the amplitude, phase or frequency of a carrier signal**; the signal occupies a **band around the carrier frequency**; common for **wireless and optical** channels. Techniques: **ASK** (two amplitudes), **FSK** (two or more frequencies), **PSK** (BPSK 0°/180°, QPSK four phases, 2 bits/symbol), **QAM-16** (4 bits/symbol), **QAM-64** (6 bits/symbol). Examples: WiFi, cellular, modems, DSL.

## D. ASK, FSK and the efficiency of QAM-16

> **2022/23 Q4(c):** Describe how ASK and FSK are used to transmit binary signals. How efficient is QAM-16 compared to binary ASK/FSK?

### Expected answer
- **ASK (Amplitude Shift Keying):** the carrier's **amplitude** changes with the data: e.g. large amplitude (carrier on) for 1, small/zero amplitude for 0; frequency and phase stay constant.
- **FSK (Frequency Shift Keying):** the carrier's **frequency** changes: one frequency (tone) for 1 and another for 0; amplitude constant.
- Both are **binary**: V = 2 → **1 bit per symbol**.
- **QAM-16** combines **amplitude and phase** to make **16 symbols → 4 bits per symbol** ($\log_2 16$).
- So at the same symbol rate/bandwidth QAM-16 carries **4× the data**: e.g. on a 3 kHz channel binary ASK/FSK gives $2\times3000\times1 = 6$ kbps, QAM-16 gives $2\times3000\times4 = 24$ kbps. Trade-off: QAM-16's points are closer together, so it needs a **higher SNR** (more sensitive to noise).

## E. NRZ and NRZI drawings

> **2019/20 Q4(c):** Draw a diagram describing voltage change in the transmission of following bit stream using Non-Return to Zero (NRZ) and Non-Return to Zero-Inverted (NRZI). Bit stream: 1 0 1 1 1 0 1 1 0 0 0

**Assumptions to state:** NRZ: +V = 1, −V = 0. NRZI: starts at −V; **a 1 causes a transition** at the start of the bit, **a 0 causes none**.

```text
     -V ──┘   └───┘           └───┘       └───────────
NRZI +V   ┌───────┐   ┌───┐       ┌───┐
     -V ──┘       └───┘   └───────┘   └───────────────
```
