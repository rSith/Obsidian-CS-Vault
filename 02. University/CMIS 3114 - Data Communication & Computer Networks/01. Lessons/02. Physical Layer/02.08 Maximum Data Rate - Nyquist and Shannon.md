# 02.08 · Maximum Data Rate of a Channel: Nyquist and Shannon

> [!info] [[02.00 Physical Layer|Lesson 2]] · Lecture ref Ch2 slides 78–84 · Priority 🔴 High (4/6 papers, **calculation**)
> **Asked as:** 6 MHz TV channel, 6-level signals, then 30 dB (2018/19 Q3d) · 4 MHz cable channel with QAM-16 / QAM-64 (2018/19 Q4a) · 8 kHz QAM-16, then 30 dB (2019/20 Q4d) · 3 kHz binary "cannot exceed 6000 bps", then 40 dB (2022/23 Q3d) · 4 kHz QAM-64, then 20 dB (2023/24 Q4c) · dog with tapes vs 150 Mbps line (2021/22 Q4c)

## 1. Background ideas

### Fourier analysis
*"Information can be transmitted on wires by varying some physical property such as voltage or current. By representing the value of this voltage or current as a single-valued function of time, g(t), we can model the behavior of the signal and analyze it mathematically."* Fourier showed any periodic signal is a **sum of sine waves (harmonics)** of different frequencies.

### Bandwidth-limited signals
- Signals **lose power** during transmission. If all frequencies were reduced equally, the signal would just get smaller; but *"all transmission facilities diminish different signal components by different amounts, thus introducing **distortion**."*
- A wire passes frequencies from 0 up to a **cutoff frequency** f mostly undiminished; higher frequencies are attenuated.
- **Bandwidth:** *"The width of the frequency range transmitted without being strongly attenuated."* In practice it is measured from 0 to the frequency where the **received power has fallen by half**.
- Fewer harmonics get through a narrow channel → the square wave of bits gets rounded → there is a **maximum rate** at which bits can be sent.

## 2. Nyquist: noiseless channel

*"As early as 1924, an AT&T engineer, Henry (Harry) Nyquist, realized that **even a perfect channel has a finite transmission capacity**."*

$$
\boxed{\;\text{Maximum data rate} = 2B\log_2 V \ \text{bits/sec}\;}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| $B$ | Bandwidth of the channel | Hz (convert kHz → ×10³, MHz → ×10⁶) |
| $V$ | Number of **discrete signal levels/symbols** | — |
| $\log_2 V$ | Bits carried per symbol | bits |
| $2B$ | Maximum symbols (samples) per second | baud |

**When to use:** the question says **noiseless**, or gives the **number of levels / modulation scheme** (binary, BPSK, QPSK, QAM-16, QAM-64…).

**V for each scheme:** binary/BPSK/ASK/FSK → 2 · QPSK → 4 · QAM-16 → 16 · QAM-64 → 64.

### Lecture example
Noiseless channel, **B = 3 kHz**, binary signals:
$2 \times 3000 \times \log_2 2 = 2 \times 3000 \times 1 = 6000$ bps = **6 kbps**.
Same channel: BPSK = 6 kbps · QPSK ($\log_2 4 = 2$) = **12 kbps** · QAM-16 ($\log_2 16 = 4$) = **24 kbps** · QAM-64 ($\log_2 64 = 6$) = **36 kbps**.

## 3. Signal-to-noise ratio and decibels
*"There is always random (**thermal**) noise present due to the motion of the molecules."* Noise is measured by the **SNR = S/N** (signal power ÷ noise power), usually quoted in **decibels**:

$$
\text{SNR}_{\text{dB}} = 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{S}{N}\right)
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\frac{S}{N} = 10^{\,\text{dB}/10}
$$

| S/N | dB |
|---|---|
| 10 | 10 |
| 100 | 20 |
| 1000 | 30 |
| 10,000 | 40 |

> [!warning] Most common mistake
> **Never put the dB value straight into Shannon's formula.** Convert first: 30 dB → S/N = 1000, not 30.

## 4. Shannon: noisy channel
*"In 1948, Claude Shannon carried Nyquist's work further and extended it to the case of a channel subject to random noise… the most important paper in all of information theory."*

$$
\boxed{\;C = B\log_2\!\left(1 + \frac{S}{N}\right)\ \text{bits/sec}\;}
$$

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| $C$ | Maximum data rate (**capacity**) of the noisy channel |
| $B$ | Bandwidth (Hz) |
| $S/N$ | Signal-to-noise **ratio** (a plain number, not dB) |

**Shannon does not depend on V**: no matter how many levels you use, you cannot beat C.

### Which limit applies?
- Noiseless + given V → **Nyquist only**.
- Noisy + no V given → **Shannon only**.
- Noisy **and** a modulation scheme given → compute **both**; the achievable rate is the **smaller** of the two (Nyquist limits what *that scheme* can do; Shannon limits what *any scheme* can do).

**Computing $\log_2$ on a calculator:** $\log_2 x = \dfrac{\log_{10} x}{\log_{10} 2} = \dfrac{\log_{10} x}{0.3010}$.

## 5. Worked past-paper solutions

### 2018/19 Q3(d): TV channel 6 MHz
**(iii) six-level signals, noiseless.** Given B = 6 MHz, V = 6.
$$2 \times 6\times10^{6} \times \log_2 6 = 12\times10^{6} \times 2.585 = 31.02\times10^{6}\ \text{bps} \approx \mathbf{31\ Mbps}$$
**(iv) SNR 30 dB.** S/N = $10^{30/10}$ = 1000.
$$C = 6\times10^{6}\log_2(1001) = 6\times10^{6}\times 9.967 \approx \mathbf{59.8\ Mbps}$$

### 2018/19 Q4(a): cable channel 4 MHz
- **QAM-16:** $2 \times 4\times10^6 \times \log_2 16 = 8\times10^6 \times 4 = \mathbf{32\ Mbps}$
- **QAM-64:** $2 \times 4\times10^6 \times \log_2 64 = 8\times10^6 \times 6 = \mathbf{48\ Mbps}$

### 2019/20 Q4(d): noiseless 8 kHz channel, QAM-16
**(i)** $2 \times 8000 \times \log_2 16 = 16000 \times 4 = \mathbf{64\ kbps}$
**(ii) SNR 30 dB** → S/N = 1000: $C = 8000 \times \log_2 1001 = 8000 \times 9.967 \approx \mathbf{79.7\ kbps}$.
**Interpretation:** the noise limit (79.7 kbps) is **above** the QAM-16 limit (64 kbps), so with QAM-16 the channel still achieves **64 kbps**; Shannon says no scheme could exceed ≈ 79.7 kbps on this noisy channel.

### 2022/23 Q3(d): noiseless 3 kHz binary channel
**(i) Show it cannot exceed 6000 bps:** binary ⇒ V = 2: $2 \times 3000 \times \log_2 2 = 6000$ bps. Nyquist's formula is the **maximum** for a noiseless channel, so the rate **cannot exceed 6000 bps**.
**(ii) 40 dB noise** → S/N = $10^{4}$ = 10,000:
$C = 3000 \times \log_2(10001) = 3000 \times 13.288 \approx \mathbf{39.86\ kbps}$.
**Interpretation:** Shannon allows up to ≈ 39.9 kbps with a better (multi-level) signalling scheme; with **binary** signals the channel is still limited to **6 kbps** by Nyquist. State both.

### 2023/24 Q4(c): noiseless 4 kHz channel, QAM-64
**(i)** $2 \times 4000 \times \log_2 64 = 8000 \times 6 = \mathbf{48\ kbps}$
**(ii) SNR 20 dB** → S/N = 100: $C = 4000 \times \log_2 101 = 4000 \times 6.658 \approx \mathbf{26.6\ kbps}$.
**Interpretation:** noise **reduces** the maximum from 48 kbps to about **26.6 kbps**; QAM-64 cannot reach its 48 kbps on this noisy channel (the constellation points would be too close to distinguish).

### Lecture example (slide 83) and a source conflict
*"A noiseless 4-kHz channel is sampled every 1 msec. What is the maximum data rate?"* The slide works it as $2 \times 4000 \times \log_2 1000$ (treating 1000 samples/sec as 1000 states) ≈ 79.7 kbps, and with **30 dB** noise $4000 \times \log_2 1001 \approx 39.9$ kbps.
> [!warning] Genuine conflict with the textbook
> In Tanenbaum this is a problem whose textbook answer is different: a **noiseless** channel can carry an arbitrarily large amount of information because each sample can carry many bits; it depends on V, not on the sampling interval. The lecture's working mixes "samples per second" with "levels". For the exam, **follow the formula $2B\log_2 V$ with the V the question gives**, and use Shannon for the noisy part (39.9 kbps), which both sources agree on.

### 2021/22 Q4(c): the dog and the tapes (bandwidth vs delay)
*Dog carries 3 tapes × 10 GB at 18 km/h. For what distances does the dog beat a 150 Mbps line?*

**Given → Formula → Substitution → Answer**
- Data carried: $3 \times 10\ \text{GB} = 30\ \text{GB} = 30 \times 10^9 \times 8 = 2.4\times10^{11}$ bits.
- Line time to send the same data: $t = \dfrac{2.4\times10^{11}}{150\times10^{6}} = 1600\ \text{s}$.
- Dog's travel time for distance $d$: $t_{dog} = \dfrac{d}{18}\ \text{h} = \dfrac{d}{18}\times 3600\ \text{s} = 200d\ \text{s}$ (d in km).
- Dog is faster when $200d < 1600 \Rightarrow d < \mathbf{8\ km}$.

| Change | New break-even distance | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (i) Dog's speed doubled (36 km/h) | **16 km** | Dog covers twice the distance in the same time |
| (ii) Tape capacity doubled (60 GB) | **16 km** | Line now needs 3200 s |
| (iii) Line rate doubled (300 Mbps) | **4 km** | Line now needs only 800 s |

*(If GB is taken as $2^{30}$ bytes, the break-even is ≈ 8.6 km; state your assumption.)* Lesson: physical transport (**magnetic media**, [[02.01 Guided Transmission Media]]) has huge bandwidth but large delay.

## 6. Common mistakes
- Using kHz/MHz without converting to Hz (or mixing units in the answer).
- Using dB directly in Shannon.
- Using $\log_{10}$ instead of $\log_2$.
- Forgetting the **2** in Nyquist.
- Forgetting to say which limit is **binding** when both are asked.
