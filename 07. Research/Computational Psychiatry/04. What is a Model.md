# What is a Model?

Back to [[00. Computational Psychiatry - Research Home|Home]] · Previous: [[03. Theory-driven vs Data-driven Approaches]] · Source: paper pp. 3–5

## 1. What is a model?
A model is a **simplified representation of a complex system**. It doesn't have to be mathematical.

For example, Freud's model of the mind (id, ego and superego) was a model made of ideas, with no maths.

In science, models are used to **organise data** and to **describe, predict or explain** how a system behaves.

## 2. Why psychiatry is hard to model
Two ideas make psychiatry difficult:

| Concept | Simple meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Equifinality** | Different causes → same result | Two people have the same symptom for different underlying reasons |
| **Multifinality** | Same cause → different results | Two people with similar childhood maltreatment develop different conditions |

## 3. Statistical models and their limits
Statistical models, such as **linear regression** (fitting a straight line), link measured variables with a formula. They are simple and easy to interpret, but:
- they show **association, not causation**;
- they struggle with **non-linear** (curved) relationships.

**Example from the paper:** a straight line could relate dopamine levels in a brain reward area to **anhedonia** (reduced ability to feel pleasure). But it wouldn't explain the process, wouldn't prove cause, and wrongly assumes a straight-line relationship.

Biology is usually **non-linear**. A moderate level of brain activity tends to be best, and too little or too much leads to problems.

## 4. Computational models
A **computational model** is a mathematical representation of a biological or psychological process. It is built to overcome the limits above.

- In **theory-driven** work, it describes how the data are produced.
- In **data-driven** work, it captures the key patterns in the data.

It can **simulate** a system to see what happens when something changes.

**Example from the paper: panic disorder.** One model treats panic disorder as a system of symptoms that feed each other:
- body arousal (racing heart) ↔ feeling threatened → a self-amplifying **panic attack**;
- avoidance and beliefs about danger → symptoms **last longer**.

## 5. Parameters
A **parameter** is a number in the model that summarises a hidden process. You can't observe it directly; you estimate it from data.
- In a straight line $y = ax + b$, the slope $a$ and intercept $b$ are parameters.
- In psychiatry models, a parameter might be **how quickly someone updates their beliefs** or **how much information they need before deciding**.

## 6. Error and model fitting
| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **Error** | The gap between the model's prediction and the real data |
| **Model fitting** | Adjusting the parameters to make the error as small as possible |
| **Model comparison** | Testing several models on the same data and choosing the best balance of fit and simplicity |

## 7. Generalisation and overfitting
A good model must predict **new, unseen data**, such as behaviour outside the lab.

**Overfitting** happens when a model fits its training data too closely and then fails on new data.

| Model | Result |
|---|---|
| Too simple | Misses the pattern (**underfitting**) |
| Just right | Captures the real pattern and works on new data |
| Too complex | Memorises noise (**overfitting**) |
