> [!info] Extra context (not from the paper): what a "narrative review" is
> A **narrative review** summarises a field in the authors' own words, using selected examples. It is *not* a **systematic review**, which follows a fixed, reproducible search-and-selection protocol. So this paper is a guided tour of CP, not a complete survey. It reports no new experiments. Every number it gives (for example "80–90% accuracy") comes from other studies it cites.

## What the paper is trying to do

> [!quote] From the paper (p. 1, Summary)
> The aim is "to provide an introductory overview of Computational Psychiatry (CP)": a broad, interdisciplinary field that combines mathematics, informatics, neuroscience and related disciplines to **improve the understanding, prognosis, and treatment of psychiatric disorders**.

**Prognosis** means predicting how an illness will develop over time.

## The abstract in plain English

1. **The problem.** Psychiatric research has long-standing weaknesses: no formal theories, diagnostic systems that only describe symptoms, few reliable biological markers, overly simple ways of measuring outcomes, treatments that aren't targeted to the person, and many findings that fail to replicate. → [[02. Why Psychiatry Needs Computational Methods]]
2. **Two strategies.**
   - *Theory-driven* approaches build **generative models**, often based on cognitive neuroscience, that describe hidden mental processes as mathematics. Examples include belief updating, decision-making and reinforcement learning. They aim to explain how symptoms *emerge* from altered computations in the brain, connecting biology, behaviour and subjective experience. → [[05. Theory-driven Computational Models]]
   - *Data-driven* approaches use machine learning on large datasets that combine many kinds of data ("multimodal"). They find hidden structure that supports **disease subtyping** (splitting a disorder into subgroups), **risk stratification** (sorting people by risk level), **predicting clinical trajectories** (how a patient's illness will progress) and **estimating differential treatment response** (which treatment will work for whom). → [[10. Data-driven Modelling - AI and Machine Learning]]
3. **Together** they help generate and test hypotheses and move towards **precision psychiatry**: treatment tailored to a person's causes ("etiological"), clinical picture, and disease biology ("pathophysiology"). → [[03. Theory-driven vs Data-driven Approaches]]
4. **Examples used in the paper:** reinforcement learning models of anhedonia, drift diffusion models of evidence accumulation, and modern AI (deep learning, explainable AI). → [[06. Reinforcement Learning and the Rescorla-Wagner Model]], [[08. Drift Diffusion Models]], [[09. Probabilistic Reward Task - Anhedonia Case Study]]
5. **Challenges:** generalisability, interpretability, ethical oversight, and integration into routine clinical practice. → [[13. Clinical Use - Promises, Challenges and Explainable AI]]

## Key takeaways

1. CP does **not** aim to replace the clinician's conversation with the patient. It aims to *formalise and enrich* it with numbers that can be measured (p. 2).
2. Current diagnostic manuals (DSM-5, ICD-11) are designed to be **reliable** (consistent), not **valid** (capturing real causes) (p. 2).
3. A model is a simplification. Good models are "as simple as possible, but not simpler" and must **generalise to unseen data** (pp. 2, 4–5).
4. Theory-driven models are **generative**: they can simulate fake behaviour, which forces researchers to state their assumptions openly and lets them run "what-if" (counterfactual) experiments (p. 5).
5. Model **parameters** (such as a person's learning rate) are hidden quantities estimated for each individual. They can show that two people with the same symptoms have different underlying mechanisms (pp. 4–5).
6. Data-driven ML can already separate schizophrenia patients from healthy people with about **80–90% accuracy**, but does worse for depression. Its most genuine clinical use so far is **early identification** of people at risk (p. 8).
7. Theory and data approaches form a **cycle**: data suggests hypotheses, and theory gives explanations that data can test (p. 3).
8. Main obstacles: **generalisability, black-box AI (hence XAI), cost and infrastructure, equitable access, algorithmic bias, privacy, over-reliance on automation**, and the deeper worry that parameters are only *proxies* for subjective experience (pp. 9–10).
