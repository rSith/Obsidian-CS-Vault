# Introduction: What is Computational Psychiatry?

Back to [[00. Computational Psychiatry - Research Home|Home]] · Source: paper pp. 1–2

## 1. What is Computational Psychiatry?
Computational Psychiatry (CP) is a field that uses **mathematics, statistics, computer science and neuroscience** to understand mental disorders better.

It tries to understand not only **what** symptoms a person has, but also **how and why** those symptoms develop.

For example, instead of only observing that a person with depression feels hopeless, researchers might study how that person processes information, learns from experiences and makes decisions.

The main goals are to:
- understand mental disorders more deeply,
- improve diagnosis,
- predict how a disorder may develop,
- choose treatments that suit each individual,
- improve patient outcomes.

## 2. Why do we need Computational Psychiatry?
Traditional psychiatry often relies on:
- patient interviews,
- questionnaires,
- doctors' observations and clinical judgement.

These matter because mental health involves a person's own experiences. But they can lead to different diagnoses, uncertain predictions, and treatments that work for some patients but not others.

For example, two people may both be diagnosed with depression. One mainly feels hopeless; the other mainly struggles with sleep and motivation. They may respond differently to the same treatment.

CP combines these clinical observations with mathematical models and measurable data to understand such differences.

> **Important:** CP does not aim to replace interviews or doctors. It aims to support and improve clinical judgement with quantitative methods.

## 3. Why are mathematical models needed?
Mental disorders are complex. They involve several connected levels:

| Level | Examples |
|---|---|
| Biological | Brain activity and neurobiology |
| Psychological | Thoughts, emotions and beliefs |
| Behavioural, social and environmental | Actions, relationships and life experiences |

These levels influence one another, so a mental disorder cannot be explained by looking at just one of them. CP uses mathematical models to represent these relationships and test whether an explanation fits the evidence.

The paper gives an important principle:

> Models should be **"as simple as possible, but not simpler"**.

In simple English: a model should be easy to understand, but it must still capture the important parts of the real problem and make correct predictions.

## 4. Limitations of traditional diagnostic systems
Two major diagnostic systems:
- **DSM-5**: Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders.
- **ICD-11**: International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision.

They classify mental disorders by **which symptoms occur together**. The paper criticises them because they describe symptoms rather than explain the mechanisms that cause them.

For example, depression is diagnosed from a combination of symptoms. But the label alone doesn't explain why one person develops depression, or why a treatment works for one person and not another.

| Concept | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **Reliability** | Does the method give consistent results? |
| **Validity** | Does it correctly measure what it is supposed to (the real cause)? |

The paper argues that diagnostic categories **prioritise reliability over validity**. They were designed to be consistent, not to identify causes. It also says psychiatry still lacks formal, testable theories.

## 5. What is RDoC?
**RDoC** (Research Domain Criteria) is an initiative of the US **National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH)**.

It moves beyond diagnostic labels. It organises research around **functional domains**: basic brain-and-behaviour functions, such as how people process information or respond to rewards.

However, the paper says RDoC and similar approaches still need stronger theories that connect:
- clinical symptoms,
- psychological and behavioural processes,
- underlying biology.

**Open science** (sharing data, transparent methods) is also valuable. But the paper argues that transparency alone cannot fill the missing theory.

## 6. What does a computational model do?
A computational model uses mathematics or computer algorithms to represent a process and test ideas (hypotheses) about it.

A key idea is the **latent mechanism**: a hidden process that cannot be observed directly but can be estimated from observable evidence.

Example:
- **Observable data:** a person keeps choosing an option that gives an immediate reward.
- **Possible latent mechanism:** the person gives more weight to immediate rewards than to future ones.

Researchers build a model and check whether it explains the person's choices.

Computational models make their **assumptions and uncertainty explicit**, so explanations can be tested, rejected or improved.

They can also analyse **large, complex datasets** to find patterns even when researchers don't start with a theory. Faster computers made this possible.
