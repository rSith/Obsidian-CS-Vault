# Drift Diffusion Models (DDM)

Back to [[00. Computational Psychiatry - Research Home|Home]] · Previous: [[07. Bayesian Brain and Belief Updating]] · Source: paper pp. 7–8

## 1. What is a DDM?
A DDM explains how we make a **quick choice between two options** by **collecting evidence over time** until we feel sure enough.

It is used for simple perception tasks with **no feedback**, such as "Was the line long or short?"

## 2. How it works
Imagine a marker that starts in the middle between two lines:
- Each moment, evidence pushes the marker slightly towards one answer.
- The path is **noisy**, so it zig-zags.
- When the marker reaches one of the lines (a **boundary**), you make that choice.

## 3. The main parts
| Part | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| **Drift rate (v)** | Strength of the evidence. Stronger evidence means faster, more accurate decisions |
| **Boundary (a)** | How much evidence you need before deciding |
| **Starting point (z)** | Where you start; starting closer to one side means a bias towards that answer |
| **Non-decision time** | Time to see the stimulus and press the button |

**Reaction time** = non-decision time + decision time

The random noise explains why people sometimes make mistakes and why their reaction times vary.

## 4. Why DDMs are useful
DDMs match how the brain works. Groups of neurons **add up evidence until they reach a firing threshold**, just like the model reaches a boundary. So DDMs **link decision theory to real brain activity**.

## 5. Use in psychiatry
DDMs can be applied to the **Probabilistic Reward Task**. There they show how learned reward bias affects each decision. → [[09. Probabilistic Reward Task - Anhedonia Case Study]]
