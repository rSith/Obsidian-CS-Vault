# Drift Diffusion Models (DDMs)

Back to [[00. Computational Psychiatry - Research Home|Research Home]] · Previous: [[07. Bayesian Brain and Belief Updating]] · Source: [[01. Paper - What is Computational Psychiatry (Antonucci 2026)|Antonucci 2026]], pp. 7–8

## Where DDMs fit

RL models explain how we **learn from reward and punishment**. The paper says other models are a better fit for **psychophysics experiments** and for studying how **evidence accumulation** works and how it is impaired (p. 7).

**Psychophysics** studies how physical stimuli (brightness, length, sound) relate to what we perceive. A typical task: "Was the line long or short?"

> [!quote] From the paper (p. 7)
> "Drift Diffusion Models, for example, are a useful approach to **simple two-choice tasks** in which **feedback is not given** but the responses are based on the **perceptual characteristic of the stimulus**."

| | RL models | DDMs |
|---|---|---|
| Task type | Learning over many trials with feedback | Two-choice perceptual decisions, no feedback needed |
| Main data used | Which option was chosen | Which option was chosen **and how long it took** (reaction time) |
| Question answered | How do expectations change with reward? | How is a single decision formed over time? |

## How a DDM works

> [!quote] From the paper (p. 7)
> "DDMs assume that decision-making involves the **noisy accumulation of evidence over time**, represented as a 'drift' toward one of two boundaries corresponding to the possible choices. The **speed of this drift reflects the strength of the evidence**, while the **boundary distance reflects the decision threshold**. **Random variability** in the accumulation process accounts for differences in both accuracy and reaction times."

Imagine a counter that starts in the middle. Each moment you glance at the stimulus, the counter moves a little towards "long" or "short". On average it moves the right way, but noise makes it zig-zag. As soon as it hits one of the two boundaries, you respond with that choice.

### The parameters (from Figure 2D)

| Symbol | Name | Meaning | What changes if it's bigger |
|---|---|---|---|
| $v$ | **Drift rate** | Average speed and direction of evidence accumulation = **strength of evidence** | Faster and more accurate decisions |
| $a$ | **Boundary separation** (decision threshold) | Distance between the two decision boundaries. In Fig. 2D the upper boundary $a$ = correct and the lower boundary $0$ = error | More evidence needed, so slower but more careful decisions *(see note below)* |
| $z$ | **Starting point** | Where accumulation begins. Closer to one boundary = a **bias** towards that choice | A built-in lean towards one answer |
| — | **Non-decision time** | Time for **perceptual encoding** (seeing the stimulus) and **motor execution** (pressing the button), which are not part of deciding | Adds a constant delay |

> [!quote] From the paper (Figure 2D caption)
> "**Reaction time (RT)** comprises non-decision time (perceptual encoding and motor execution) plus decision time. The distribution at each boundary represents the reaction time for correct responses and error."

$$\text{RT} = \text{non-decision time} + \text{decision time}$$

> [!info] Extra context (not from the paper)
> - The "what changes" column for $a$ and $z$ uses standard DDM interpretation; the paper only gives the definitions. A wider boundary means a **speed–accuracy trade-off**: slower but fewer errors.
> - In discrete time, one step of the accumulator is often written $x_{t+\Delta t} = x_t + v\,\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\epsilon$, with $\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$. The paper doesn't give an equation for the DDM.

## Simulation

![[cp-ddm-simulation.png]]
*My simulation, not from the paper (3,000 simulated trials). Middle: example evidence paths. Blue paths hit the "correct" boundary and orange paths hit the "error" boundary. Top and bottom: the resulting reaction-time distributions. Note the long right tail, typical of reaction times, which the DDM reproduces naturally.*

```python
import numpy as np

def ddm_trial(v=0.8, a=2.0, z=1.0, t0=0.3, sigma=1.0, dt=0.002, rng=np.random.default_rng()):
    """Simulate one decision. Returns (choice, reaction_time)."""
    x, t = z, 0.0
    while 0 < x < a:                       # keep accumulating until a boundary is hit
        x += v * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.normal()
        t += dt
    return ("correct" if x >= a else "error"), t + t0   # add non-decision time
```

Panel D shows (from the caption):
- The **stimulus** arrives, followed by a short perceptual **non-decision time**.
- Evidence starts at **z** and drifts with rate **v** (green arrow) along a noisy path (red line) until it reaches **a** (correct decision) or **0** (error decision, blue path).
- After the boundary, there is a **motor non-decision time** and then the **response**.
- Above boundary **a** is the **correct RT distribution**, and below 0 is the (smaller) **error RT distribution**.

## Why DDMs are attractive: they match the brain

> [!quote] From the paper (p. 7)
> "Although originally developed as mathematical models of human decision-making, DDMs have also proven valid in describing **neural mechanisms**. In particular, the accumulation-to-boundary process in DDMs closely parallels the way **populations of neurons integrate incoming evidence until a firing-rate threshold is reached, triggering a spike or action potential**." This convergence "highlights the value of DDMs as a **bridge between abstract decision theory and concrete brain dynamics**."

- **Firing rate**: how often a neuron sends electrical signals.
- **Action potential / spike**: the electrical pulse a neuron fires.

> [!info] Extra context (not from the paper)
> The paper's reference for this (ref. 53, Shadlen & Newsome 2001) studied neurons in the **lateral intraparietal area (LIP)** of monkeys while they made perceptual decisions. That's where the "neurons ramp up to a threshold" picture comes from.

## DDMs in psychiatry

The paper applies DDMs to the **Probabilistic Reward Task**. Because the PRT is a two-choice task with objectively correct answers and measurable errors, it "lends itself naturally" to DDMs, which can capture "not only the overall accuracy and reaction times, but also **trial-by-trial dynamics of how biased reinforcement learning influences evidence accumulation**" (pp. 7–8, ref. 65). → [[09. Probabilistic Reward Task - Anhedonia Case Study]]

> The paper doesn't say how RL and DDM are combined mathematically (for example, which DDM parameter the learned reward bias affects).
