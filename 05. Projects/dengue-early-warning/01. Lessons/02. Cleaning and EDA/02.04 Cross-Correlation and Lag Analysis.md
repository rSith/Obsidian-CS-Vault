# 02.04 · Cross-Correlation and Lag Analysis

> [!info] [[02.00 Cleaning and EDA|Lesson 2]] · Roadmap Phase 2 · Priority 🔴 Core
> **Used for:** the central finding of Phase 2: how many weeks after rain, warmth and humidity do cases follow, in each district?

## 1. Definition
> [!note] Cross-correlation
> **Cross-correlation** is the correlation between **two different series** when one of them is shifted in time. At lag $k$ it compares weather from $k$ weeks ago with cases now.

$$r_{xy}(k) = \operatorname{corr}\left(x_{t-k},\; y_t\right)$$

Here $x$ is the weather series, $y$ is the case series, and $k$ is the lag in weeks. [[02.03 Time-Series Basics - Trend, Seasonality and Autocorrelation|Autocorrelation]] is the same idea with one series compared with itself.

## 2. Why it is used
Biology says cases follow weather by "some weeks" ([[00.03 Dengue Transmission and the Mosquito Life Cycle]]). The model needs a number. Lag analysis finds it from the data, separately for each district, so the features in Phase 3 are chosen by evidence.

## 3. How it works
1. Take the weather series and shift it by $k$ weeks.
2. Compute the correlation between the shifted weather and the cases.
3. Repeat for $k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 16$.
4. Plot correlation against $k$. The lag with the **highest** correlation is the delay at which that weather variable relates most strongly to cases.

## 4. Syntax
```python
import pandas as pd

def lag_correlations(g: pd.DataFrame, x: str, y: str = "cases", max_lag: int = 16) -> pd.Series:
    g = g.sort_values("week_start")
    return pd.Series({k: g[y].corr(g[x].shift(k)) for k in range(max_lag + 1)})

ccf_rain = weekly.groupby("district").apply(lag_correlations, x="rain_mm")
ccf_rain.T.plot(marker="o", xlabel="Lag (weeks)", ylabel="Correlation with cases")
```
`ccf_rain` has one row per district and one column per lag.

## 5. Example
| Lag (weeks) | 0 | 2 | 4 | 6 | 8 | 10 | 12 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Correlation, rain vs cases (Colombo) | 0.05 | 0.14 | 0.27 | 0.38 | **0.41** | 0.33 | 0.20 |

Reading: *"In Colombo, rainfall 6–8 weeks earlier is most strongly related to cases."* That is a finding to write at the end of the notebook. The numbers above are made up to show the shape.

## 6. From an analysis lag to a feature lag
The lag you find is counted back from the **case week**. Features are counted back from the **forecast week**, which is $h$ weeks earlier.

$$\text{feature lag} = \text{analysis lag} - h$$

| Analysis lag | Forecasting $h$ weeks ahead | Feature lag |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | 4 | rain **4** weeks before the forecast week |
| 8 | 8 | rain from the forecast week itself |
| 6 | 8 | −2: that rain has **not fallen yet** |

The last row is why forecasting 8 weeks ahead is hard: the most useful weather is still in the future. See [[03.01 Forecast Origin, Horizon and Target]].

## 7. Traps in reading the result
> [!warning] Shared seasonality inflates correlation
> Rain and cases both follow the yearly calendar. Two seasonal series correlate at many lags even with no direct link. To test for a link beyond the calendar, remove the usual seasonal level first and correlate what is left (the **anomalies**):
> ```python
> woy = weekly["week_start"].dt.isocalendar().week
> for col in ["cases", "rain_mm"]:
>     normal = weekly.groupby(["district", woy])[col].transform("mean")
>     weekly[col + "_anom"] = weekly[col] - normal
> ```
> A peak that survives in the anomalies is much stronger evidence.

- **Correlation is not causation.** It shows that two things move together, not that one causes the other.
- **Case counts are skewed.** A few huge weeks dominate Pearson correlation. Use `np.log1p(cases)`, or Spearman: `g[y].corr(g[x].shift(k), method="spearman")`.
- **Noise.** With $n$ weeks of data, a correlation smaller than about $2/\sqrt{n}$ is not distinguishable from zero. For 400 weeks that is about $0.1$.
- **Many tries.** Testing 17 lags for 3 variables in 25 districts gives over a thousand numbers. Some will be high by chance. Look for a **smooth hump**, not one lonely spike.
- **The relationship may not be a straight line.** Very heavy rain can reduce breeding. Correlation measures only straight-line association.

## 8. Common mistakes
- **Shifting the wrong series or the wrong way.** `x.shift(k)` puts weather from $k$ weeks ago beside this week's cases, which is what you want.
- **Pooling all districts in one calculation.** Each district has its own lag.
- **Reporting only the single best lag.** Neighbouring lags are nearly as good; report a range such as "6–8 weeks".
- **Using the lag from analysis directly as a feature lag** without subtracting the horizon.
- **Treating a high correlation as proof** that the feature will improve the forecast. Only the model comparison in Phase 4 shows that.

## 9. Exam and viva focus
- **Define** cross-correlation and write $r_{xy}(k) = \operatorname{corr}(x_{t-k}, y_t)$.
- **Autocorrelation vs cross-correlation:** one series with itself vs two different series.
- **What does the peak of the plot tell you?** The delay at which the two series are most related.
- **Why can two seasonal series give a misleading correlation?** Both follow the calendar.
- **Pearson vs Spearman:** linear relationship on values vs relationship on ranks; Spearman is less affected by extreme values.
- **Correlation vs causation.**
