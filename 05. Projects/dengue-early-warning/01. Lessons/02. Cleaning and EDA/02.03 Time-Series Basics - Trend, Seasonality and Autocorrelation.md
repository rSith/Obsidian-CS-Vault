# 02.03 · Time-Series Basics: Trend, Seasonality and Autocorrelation

> [!info] [[02.00 Cleaning and EDA|Lesson 2]] · Roadmap Phase 2 · Priority 🔴 Core
> **Used for:** understanding the shape of the case data before modelling, and explaining why "recent cases" is usually the strongest feature.

## 1. Definition
> [!note] Time series
> A **time series** is a set of values measured at regular points in time and kept **in order**: weekly cases, daily rainfall. The order carries information, so the rows cannot be shuffled.

## 2. Why it is used
Ordinary machine learning assumes each row is independent of the others. Weekly dengue counts are not: this week depends on last week. Knowing how lets you choose features, choose baselines and split the data correctly.

## 3. The parts of a time series
| Part | Meaning | In dengue data |
|---|---|---|
| **Trend** ($T$) | Slow, long-term rise or fall | More cases over the years as cities grow |
| **Seasonality** ($S$) | A pattern that repeats with a fixed period | Peaks around June–July and December–January every year |
| **Cycle** | Rises and falls with no fixed length | Epidemic years every few years |
| **Remainder** ($R$) | What is left: noise and surprises | A sudden outbreak, a reporting error |

Two ways to combine them:

$$\text{Additive: } y_t = T_t + S_t + R_t \qquad\qquad \text{Multiplicative: } y_t = T_t \times S_t \times R_t$$

- **Additive:** the seasonal swing is the same size every year.
- **Multiplicative:** the swing grows when the level grows. Case counts usually behave this way: a bad year has a much bigger peak.
- Taking a logarithm turns multiplicative into additive: $\log y = \log T + \log S + \log R$. Use `np.log1p(cases)` so that zero is allowed.

## 4. Autocorrelation
> [!note] Autocorrelation
> **Autocorrelation** is the correlation of a series with **an earlier copy of itself**. At lag $k$ it measures how closely this week's value follows the value $k$ weeks ago.

$$r_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{n} (y_t - \bar{y})(y_{t-k} - \bar{y})}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} (y_t - \bar{y})^2}$$

- $r_k$ lies between $-1$ and $+1$.
- $r_1$ close to $1$ means this week looks a lot like last week.
- A plot of $r_k$ against $k$ is the **ACF plot** (autocorrelation function).

**Why dengue is strongly autocorrelated:** infected people infect mosquitoes, which infect more people. Cases this week are the *cause* of cases in the next few weeks.

| What the ACF plot shows | What it means |
|---|---|
| Slow decay from lag 1 | Strong persistence: use recent cases as features |
| A bump near lag 52 | Yearly seasonality |
| Bars inside the shaded band | Not distinguishable from noise |

## 5. Stationarity
A series is **stationary** when its mean and variance do not change over time. Classical methods such as ARIMA need it. Tree models and regression with lag features do not strictly need it, but they still work better when the series is not drifting far outside the range seen in training.

## 6. Syntax
```python
import numpy as np
from statsmodels.tsa.seasonal import seasonal_decompose
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf

s = (weekly[weekly["district"] == "Colombo"]
     .set_index("week_start")["cases"]
     .interpolate())                       # decomposition cannot handle gaps (analysis only)

result = seasonal_decompose(np.log1p(s), model="additive", period=52)
result.plot()                              # observed, trend, seasonal, residual

plot_acf(s, lags=60)                       # autocorrelation up to 60 weeks
```
`period=52` tells the function that the pattern repeats every 52 weeks.

## 7. Example: reading a decomposition
Suppose the plot for Colombo shows:
- **Trend:** flat, with one large hump in 2017 and a dip in 2020–21.
- **Seasonal:** two bumps a year, the mid-year one larger.
- **Residual:** small most of the time, large in 2017.

In words: *"Cases in Colombo follow a two-peak yearly pattern. 2017 was far above the usual level and 2020–21 far below. Outside those years, the seasonal pattern explains most of the variation."* That sentence belongs in your findings.

## 8. Common mistakes
- **Shuffling rows or splitting randomly.** It destroys the order: [[04.01 Time-Based Splits and Time-Series Cross-Validation]].
- **Wrong period.** `period=12` is for monthly data. Weekly data with a yearly pattern needs 52.
- **Decomposing a series with gaps.** Fill the gaps first, and only for this analysis.
- **Confusing seasonality with a cycle.** Seasonality has a fixed period; a cycle does not.
- **Reading meaning into small ACF bars** that lie inside the confidence band.
- **Using an additive model on raw counts** when the peaks grow with the level. Use the log.
