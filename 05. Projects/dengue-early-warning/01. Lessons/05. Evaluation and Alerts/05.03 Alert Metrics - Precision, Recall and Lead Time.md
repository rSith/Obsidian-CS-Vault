# 05.03 · Alert Metrics: Precision, Recall and Lead Time

> [!info] [[05.00 Evaluation and Alerts|Lesson 5]] · Roadmap Phase 5 · Priority 🔴 Core
> **Parent concepts:** [[Model Evaluation]] · [[Classification]]. This note covers only how the alerts are scored.

## 1. Definitions
An alert is a yes/no decision: *Warning* or *no Warning*. Each one is right or wrong in one of four ways.

| | **Outbreak really happened** | **No outbreak** |
|---|---|---|
| **Warning raised** | **True positive (TP):** caught it | **False positive (FP):** false alarm |
| **No Warning** | **False negative (FN):** missed it | **True negative (TN):** correctly quiet |

This table is the **confusion matrix**.

| Metric | Question it answers | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| **Precision** | When the system warns, how often is it right? | $\dfrac{TP}{TP + FP}$ |
| **Recall** (sensitivity) | Of the real outbreaks, how many did it catch? | $\dfrac{TP}{TP + FN}$ |
| **F1 score** | One number balancing the two | $\dfrac{2 \times \text{precision} \times \text{recall}}{\text{precision} + \text{recall}}$ |
| **Accuracy** | What share of all decisions were right? | $\dfrac{TP + TN}{TP + TN + FP + FN}$ |
| **Lead time** | How early was the warning? | Weeks from the first Warning to the outbreak |

## 2. Why they are used
MAE tells you how close the numbers are. It does not tell you whether the system would have **warned people in time**, which is the purpose of the project. Two models with the same MAE can differ completely in the weeks that matter.

## 3. How they work: a worked example
100 district-weeks in the validation period. 20 were real outbreak weeks. The system raised 25 Warnings, and 15 of them were right.

| | Outbreak | No outbreak | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Warning** | TP = 15 | FP = 10 | 25 |
| **No Warning** | FN = 5 | TN = 70 | 75 |
| **Total** | 20 | 80 | 100 |

$$\text{Precision} = \frac{15}{25} = 0.60 \qquad \text{Recall} = \frac{15}{20} = 0.75 \qquad F_1 = \frac{2 \times 0.60 \times 0.75}{0.60 + 0.75} \approx 0.67$$

Reading: *"The system caught 75 % of outbreak weeks. When it warned, it was right 60 % of the time."*

## 4. Why accuracy misleads
Accuracy here is $(15 + 70)/100 = 85\,\%$. A system that **never warns** scores $80/100 = 80\,\%$, and catches nothing. Outbreak weeks are rare, so accuracy is dominated by the easy quiet weeks. This is the **class imbalance** problem. Report precision and recall.

## 5. The trade-off
Lowering the threshold makes the system warn more often.

| Threshold | Warnings | Recall | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher | Fewer | Lower: more outbreaks missed | Higher: fewer false alarms |
| Lower | More | Higher: fewer outbreaks missed | Lower: more false alarms |

Which matters more depends on the costs:

| Mistake | Cost |
|---|---|
| **Missed outbreak** (FN) | No preparation; illness that could have been prevented |
| **False alarm** (FP) | Wasted effort; after many of them, people stop listening |

For early warning a miss is usually worse, so favour **recall**, while keeping precision high enough that warnings are still believed. Choose the threshold on **validation** data, plot precision against recall for several thresholds, and state the choice.

## 6. Lead time
- Define the **start of an outbreak**: the first week actual cases cross the threshold.
- **Lead time** = the number of weeks between the system's first Warning and that start.
- Measuring to the **start** is more honest than measuring to the **peak**. A peak comes weeks after the start, so "warned before the peak" is easy to achieve.

**Count outbreaks, not weeks.** One outbreak lasting eight weeks is one event. Counting it as eight successes makes the system look better than it is. Group consecutive outbreak weeks into **episodes** and report: episodes that occurred, episodes warned in advance, and the average lead time.

## 7. Compare with a naive alert
Just as forecasts need a baseline, so do alerts. The naive alert is: *"Warn when the newest known case count is already above the threshold."* If your model does not warn **earlier** than that rule, it adds no early warning. Report both side by side.

## 8. Syntax
```python
from sklearn.metrics import confusion_matrix, precision_score, recall_score, f1_score

actual = val["target"] > val["warning"]      # real outbreak: ACTUAL cases above the line
alert  = val["pred"] > val["warning"]        # system's Warning: FORECAST above the line

tn, fp, fn, tp = confusion_matrix(actual, alert).ravel()
precision_score(actual, alert), recall_score(actual, alert), f1_score(actual, alert)
```

## 9. Common mistakes
- **Reporting accuracy** for a rare event.
- **Choosing the threshold on the test set.**
- **Counting weeks instead of outbreak episodes.**
- **Measuring lead time to the peak only.**
- **No naive alert for comparison.**
- **Mixing up precision and recall.** Precision looks at the warnings; recall looks at the outbreaks.
- **Reading much into very few events.** Three outbreaks in the test period cannot support "recall is 67 %" with any confidence. Say how many events there were.
- **Calling a warning "false"** when the outbreak began one week outside your window. Decide in advance how close counts as a hit.
