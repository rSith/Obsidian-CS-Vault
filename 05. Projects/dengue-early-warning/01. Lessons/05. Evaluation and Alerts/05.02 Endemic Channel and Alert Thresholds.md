# 05.02 · Endemic Channel and Alert Thresholds

> [!info] [[05.00 Evaluation and Alerts|Lesson 5]] · Roadmap Phase 5 · Priority 🔴 Core
> **Used for:** deciding what "unusually high" means for each district in each week, so a forecast can become Normal, Watch or Warning.

## 1. Definitions
> [!note] Endemic
> A disease is **endemic** when it is always present in a place at some expected level.

> [!note] Endemic channel
> An **endemic channel** is the range of case counts **expected for each week of the year** in a place, worked out from earlier years. Counts inside the range are normal for the season; counts above it signal a possible outbreak.

> [!note] Outbreak
> An **outbreak** is more cases than expected for that place and time.

## 2. Why it is used
300 cases in a week is quiet for Colombo in July and alarming for a small district in March. A single fixed number cannot serve as a threshold. The endemic channel gives a **different threshold for every district and every week of the year**, which is how public-health surveillance usually defines "above normal".

## 3. How it works: mean and standard deviation
For district $d$ and week of year $w$, take the counts for that week in the previous five years and compute their mean $\mu_w$ and standard deviation $\sigma_w$:

$$\text{Watch: } \hat{y} > \mu_w + \sigma_w \qquad\qquad \text{Warning: } \hat{y} > \mu_w + 2\sigma_w$$

$\hat{y}$ is the forecast for that week.

### Worked example
Colombo, week 26, the previous five years: 180, 220, 150, 260, 190.

$$\mu = \frac{180 + 220 + 150 + 260 + 190}{5} = 200$$

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{(-20)^2 + 20^2 + (-50)^2 + 60^2 + (-10)^2}{5 - 1}} = \sqrt{\frac{7000}{4}} \approx 41.8$$

| Level | Rule | Threshold |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | up to $\mu + \sigma$ | up to 242 |
| **Watch** | above $\mu + \sigma$ | above 242 |
| **Warning** | above $\mu + 2\sigma$ | above 284 |

A forecast of 270 is a **Watch**. A forecast of 300 is a **Warning**.

## 4. Four problems with the simple version, and the fixes
| Problem | Why it matters | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Only five numbers** per week | A standard deviation from five values is very unreliable, so thresholds jump up and down from week to week | **Pool neighbouring weeks:** use weeks $w-2$ to $w+2$, giving 25 values |
| **Counts are skewed** | One large year inflates both $\mu$ and $\sigma$, pushing the threshold so high that nothing triggers it | Use the **median and percentiles**, or work on $\log(1 + \text{cases})$ |
| **Epidemic years in the history** | 2017 would raise "normal" for years afterwards | **Exclude flagged epidemic years** |
| **Abnormally low years** | 2020–21 would lower "normal" and make every later year look like an outbreak | Exclude or flag those too: [[02.02 Data Quality Checks and Missing Data]] |

### The percentile version
A common alternative uses quartiles of the historical counts for each week, giving four zones:

| Zone | Range |
|---|---|
| Success | below the 25th percentile |
| Safety | 25th percentile to the median |
| Alert | median to the 75th percentile |
| Epidemic | above the 75th percentile |

Percentiles are not thrown off by one extreme year.

## 5. Syntax
```python
def endemic_channel(hist: pd.DataFrame, year: int, n_years: int = 5, window: int = 2) -> pd.DataFrame:
    """Thresholds per district and week of year, using only the n_years BEFORE `year`."""
    past = hist[(hist["year"] >= year - n_years) & (hist["year"] < year)
                & ~hist["flag_epidemic"] & ~hist["flag_covid"]]
    rows = []
    for district, g in past.groupby("district"):
        for w in range(1, 53):
            near = [((w - 1 + k) % 52) + 1 for k in range(-window, window + 1)]   # wraps 52 → 1
            v = g.loc[g["woy"].isin(near), "cases"]
            rows.append({"district": district, "woy": w,
                         "watch": v.mean() + v.std(), "warning": v.mean() + 2 * v.std(),
                         "median": v.median(), "q75": v.quantile(0.75)})
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

channel_2025 = endemic_channel(hist, year=2025)
```
Then compare each forecast with the thresholds for its district and target week:
```python
import numpy as np
df["alert"] = np.select([df["pred"] > df["warning"], df["pred"] > df["watch"]],
                        ["Warning", "Watch"], default="Normal")
```

> [!important] Only earlier years
> The channel for 2025 is built from years **before** 2025. Using all years, including later ones, lets the future decide what was "normal" in the past: [[03.03 Data Leakage]].

## 6. A forecast is smoother than reality
A model predicts the *expected* count, which is less extreme than what actually happens. So forecasts cross the Warning line less often than real counts do, and the system under-warns. Two remedies:
- lower the thresholds for forecasts a little, choosing the amount on **validation** data: [[05.03 Alert Metrics - Precision, Recall and Lead Time]];
- alert on the **chance** of exceeding the line, using [[05.04 Prediction Intervals and Quantile Regression]].

## 7. Common mistakes
- **One threshold for the whole country or the whole year.**
- **Mean and SD from five raw values**, with no pooling.
- **Including 2017, or 2020–21, without thinking.**
- **Using years after the week being judged.**
- **Tuning the thresholds on the test period.**
- **Different definitions for "alert" and for "real outbreak".** Define the real outbreak with the same thresholds applied to *actual* cases, or the scoring means nothing.
- **Thresholds on raw counts for tiny districts.** With 2 cases a week, $\mu + 2\sigma$ may be 5, and one family triggers a Warning. Set a minimum count as well.

## 8. Exam and viva focus
- **Define** endemic, epidemic, outbreak and endemic channel.
- **Endemic vs epidemic vs pandemic:** constant expected presence; a rise above the expected level in a place; an epidemic across many countries.
- **How is a threshold set with mean and standard deviation?** $\mu + 2\sigma$ of historical counts for that week.
- **Compute a threshold by hand** from five numbers.
- **Why exclude epidemic years from the baseline?** They would raise "normal" and hide later outbreaks.
- **Mean and SD vs median and percentiles:** percentiles resist extreme values.
