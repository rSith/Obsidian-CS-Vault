# 00.05 · Reading Research Papers

> [!info] [[00.00 Setup and Domain Knowledge|Lesson 0]] · Roadmap Phase 0 · Priority 🟡 Supporting
> **Used for:** the three paper summaries in `docs/literature.md`, and later the "related work" section of your report.

## 1. Definition
> [!note] Literature review
> A **literature review** is a short, organised account of what other people have already done on your problem: their data, their methods and their results.

## 2. Why it is used
- Most good feature ideas already exist in published work. Reading saves weeks of guessing.
- Papers tell you what accuracy is realistic, so you can judge your own results.
- Your report must show how your work differs from earlier work.

## 3. How it works: the three-pass method
Do not read a paper from the first word to the last. Read it in passes and stop when you have what you need.

| Pass | Time | Read | You can now answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Skim** | 5–10 min | Title, abstract, headings, figures, conclusion | Is this paper relevant to me? |
| **2. Understand** | 30–60 min | Introduction, methods, results; look hard at every figure and table | What did they do and what did they find? |
| **3. Rebuild** | Hours | Every detail, as if you had to repeat the work | Could I reproduce this? Only needed for 1–2 key papers. |

Most papers need only pass 1. Three or four need pass 2.

## 4. What to record
Fill one row of this table per paper. It is the table in `docs/literature.md`.

| Column | Question |
|---|---|
| Data | Which place, which years, weekly or monthly, how many districts? |
| Features | Rain, temperature, humidity, past cases, anything else? Which lags? |
| Model | Which models were compared? |
| Lead time | How far ahead did they predict? |
| Evaluation | How did they split the data? Which baseline? Which metric? |
| Result | The main number, and whether it beat the baseline |
| Takeaway | One thing you will copy, and one thing you will avoid |

## 5. Example
> *"Weekly cases for one district, 2010–2016. Rainfall and temperature at lags 4–12 weeks plus cases in the previous 4 weeks. Random Forest compared with ARIMA. Predicted 4 weeks ahead. Trained on 2010–2014, tested on 2015–2016. Random Forest had lower error than ARIMA. Takeaway: lagged cases were the strongest feature; they tested only one district."*

That is enough to compare this paper with yours months later.

## 6. Questions that expose weak papers
- **Was the data split by time?** A random split on time-series data lets the model see the future. See [[03.03 Data Leakage]].
- **Is there a simple baseline?** "Our model has 90 % accuracy" means nothing without a comparison. See [[04.02 Forecasting Baselines - Persistence and Seasonal]].
- **Was the model tested on years it never saw?**
- **How far ahead is the prediction really?** Predicting this week from this week's data is not early warning.
- **Is the data available in time?** See [[01.06 Reporting Delay and Data Availability]].

## 7. Common mistakes
- **Reading every word of every paper.** You run out of time after two.
- **Not writing notes.** In a month you will remember nothing.
- **Copying a result without its conditions.** "Lag 8 was best" was true for that district and that data.
- **Trusting only the abstract.** The limitations are usually near the end.
- **Losing the reference.** Save the title, authors, year and link the moment you open the paper.

## 8. Exam and viva focus
- **Purpose of a literature review:** find what is known, find the gap, justify your method.
- **Parts of a paper:** abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion.
- **What makes an evaluation trustworthy?** Time-based split, unseen test data, a baseline, a stated lead time.
