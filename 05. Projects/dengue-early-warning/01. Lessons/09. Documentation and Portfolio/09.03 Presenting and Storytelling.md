# 09.03 · Presenting and Storytelling

> [!info] [[09.00 Documentation and Portfolio|Lesson 9]] · Roadmap Phase 9 · Priority 🟡 Supporting
> **Builds on:** [[Learning in Public]]
> **Used for:** the demo video, the talk at your student branch, the LinkedIn post, and interview answers.

## 1. Definition
> [!note] Storytelling, in a technical setting
> **Storytelling** means arranging facts so that a listener can follow them and remember them: a problem, what was done about it, what happened, and what it means.

## 2. Why it is used
People forget lists of metrics and remember stories. A recruiter who hears forty project descriptions will remember "the system warned three weeks before the 2025 outbreak in Colombo" and will not remember "MAE of 39". The work is the same. The way it is told decides whether anyone recalls it.

## 3. How it works: one structure for every format
| Step | Question | For this project |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Problem** | Why should anyone care? | Dengue returns every year, and health teams learn of an outbreak when it has already started |
| **2. Approach** | What did you do? | Used weather and past cases to forecast each district 4 weeks ahead and raise alerts |
| **3. Result** | What happened? **One** striking, true fact | Your best honest result, with its comparison |
| **4. Meaning** | What did you learn, and what are the limits? | What worked, what did not, what you would do next |

The same four steps stretch or shrink to fit:

| Format | Length | Problem | Approach | Result | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| One-line pitch | 15 seconds | ½ line | ½ line | | |
| LinkedIn post | 150 words | 2 lines | 3 lines | 1 figure | 2 lines |
| Demo video | 3–5 minutes | 30 s | 60 s | 90 s | 30 s |
| Talk | 10–15 minutes | 2 min | 5 min | 4 min | 2 min |

## 4. Example: the demo video
| Time | Show | Say |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | The dashboard's map | The problem in two sentences |
| 0:30 | The architecture diagram | "Every week the system collects…, forecasts…, and alerts…" |
| 1:30 | Click a district; the chart | Explain the lines, the band and the threshold |
| 2:30 | **One historical outbreak** | "Here the system raised a Warning on this date. Cases crossed the threshold three weeks later." |
| 3:30 | A district where it failed | "Here it missed, and this is why." |
| 4:00 | The results table | The headline number against the baseline |
| 4:30 | The repository | Limits, next steps, where to find the code |

Practical points: write the script first; record the screen at a readable zoom; use a quiet room; rehearse once; keep it under five minutes.

**A one-line pitch:**
> *"I built a system that forecasts dengue cases in every Sri Lankan district four weeks ahead from weather data and raises an alert when a district is heading for an outbreak."*

**A LinkedIn post:**
> *Dengue returns to Sri Lanka every year, and by the time case numbers show an outbreak, it has already begun.*
> *Over six months I built an early-warning system: it collects weather and case data every week, forecasts each district four weeks ahead, and shows alerts on a map.*
> *The honest result: [X] % lower error than a simple baseline, and [N] of [M] outbreaks flagged in advance on unseen data. It missed [K], and I explain why in the report.*
> *What I learned most: [one real lesson].*
> *Code and report: [link]. Feedback from people in public health is very welcome.*

## 5. Answering interview questions
Use **STAR** for "tell me about a time…" questions:

| | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | The context | "Case data for my project was published seven weeks late." |
| **T**ask | What you had to do | "I needed forecasts that would still work in live use." |
| **A**ction | What **you** did | "I rebuilt the features to use only data available on the forecast day, and re-ran the evaluation under the same delay." |
| **R**esult | What happened | "The measured error rose, but it now matched what the live system could do, and weather features became more important." |

Questions to prepare for:
- "Walk me through the system." → the diagram: [[09.02 Architecture Diagrams]]
- "How do you know it works?" → baselines, time-based split, one final test
- "What was the hardest part?" → a real problem, and how you solved it
- "What would you do differently?" → shows judgement
- "Where does it fail?" → the missed outbreaks, and the limitations
- "Why this model and not a neural network?" → the comparison you ran
- "What is data leakage, and how did you avoid it?" → [[03.03 Data Leakage]]

## 6. Slides
- **One idea per slide,** stated in the title as a sentence.
- **Figures, not paragraphs.** Bring the charts from [[05.05 Charts That Communicate Results]].
- **Large text.** If it cannot be read from the back of the room, remove it.
- **About one slide per minute.**
- **End on the result and the link,** not on "Thank you".

## 7. Common mistakes
- **Starting with the technology** ("I used Flask, MySQL and scikit-learn") instead of the problem.
- **Listing everything you did** in the order you did it.
- **Too many numbers.** Choose one and make it land.
- **Overclaiming.** "Predicts outbreaks" when it flags elevated risk. One overclaim makes a listener doubt everything else.
- **Hiding the failures.** Showing one, with its cause, is what makes an audience trust the rest.
- **Reading the slides aloud.**
- **A demo that depends on the internet** in the room. Have the video as a backup.
- **Jargon for a mixed audience.** Say "how far ahead it predicts", not "horizon".
- **No clear ending or request.** Say what you want: feedback, a contact, a conversation.

## 8. Exam and viva focus
- **Structure of a technical presentation:** problem, approach, result, conclusion.
- **Know your audience:** adapt vocabulary and depth to who is listening.
- **STAR:** situation, task, action, result.
- **Qualities of a good slide.**
- **In a viva, be ready to:** explain any figure in your report, justify each design choice, and state your limitations before you are asked.
