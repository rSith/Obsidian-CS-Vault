# 09.01 · Technical and Scientific Writing

> [!info] [[09.00 Documentation and Portfolio|Lesson 9]] · Roadmap Phase 9 · Priority 🔴 Core
> **Builds on:** [[00.05 Reading Research Papers]]: you write the kind of document you learned to read.
> **Used for:** the README and the 8–15 page technical report.

## 1. Definitions
> [!note] Technical writing
> **Technical writing** explains how something works or how to use it, so that a reader can understand it or act on it.

> [!note] Scientific writing
> **Scientific writing** reports an investigation: what question was asked, how it was studied, what was found, and how far the finding can be trusted.

## 2. Why it is used
- The report turns months of work into something another person can **check and believe**.
- It can become a final-year project report or a student paper.
- Writing exposes gaps: if you cannot explain a step in a sentence, you have not finished understanding it.

## 3. How it works: the structure of a report
Most scientific reports follow **IMRaD**: Introduction, Methods, Results and Discussion.

| Section | Answers | For this project |
|---|---|---|
| **Abstract** | The whole thing in 150–250 words | Problem, data, method, main number, main limit |
| **Introduction** | Why does this matter? What exactly was done? | Dengue burden in Sri Lanka; why weeks of warning help; the question you ask |
| **Related work** | What is already known? | The papers from Phase 0, and how yours differs |
| **Data** | What did you use? | Sources, years, districts, the reporting delay, flagged periods |
| **Methods** | How could someone repeat this? | Features, splits, baselines, models, alert thresholds, metrics |
| **Results** | What did you find? | Tables and figures, with no opinion |
| **Discussion** | What does it mean? | Why weather helped or did not; which outbreaks were missed and why |
| **Limitations** | Where does it stop being true? | See section 6 |
| **Conclusion and future work** | What should happen next? | Specific next steps |
| **References** | Whose work did you use? | Papers, data sources, libraries |

## 4. The order to write in
Do not start at the introduction.
1. **Figures and tables.** They are the story.
2. **Results**, describing each figure.
3. **Methods**, saying how those results were produced.
4. **Discussion and limitations.**
5. **Introduction**, now that you know what you are introducing.
6. **Abstract**, last.

## 5. Example: the same finding, written three ways
| Version | Text | Problem |
|---|---|---|
| Vague | "The model performed well." | Compared with what? Measured how? |
| Number without context | "The model had an MAE of 39." | Is 39 good? |
| **Complete** | "At a 4-week horizon the gradient-boosting model had a validation MAE of 39 cases per week, against 52 for the persistence baseline, a reduction of 25 % (Table 3)." | |

Every claim in a results section needs four things: **the number, the unit, the comparison, and where to see the evidence.**

An **abstract** in four sentences:
> *"Dengue is a major seasonal burden in Sri Lanka, and warning of an outbreak weeks ahead would allow earlier control. We built a system that forecasts weekly cases for each of 25 districts four weeks ahead from rainfall, temperature, humidity and past cases, and raises an alert when the forecast exceeds a seasonal threshold. On unseen data from [years], the model reduced forecast error by [X] % against a persistence baseline and warned of [N] of [M] outbreaks, with a median lead time of [L] weeks. Case reports are published several weeks late, which limits the real lead time, and the system cannot anticipate serotype changes."*

## 6. Limitations to state for this project
- **Reporting delay and under-reporting.** Counts arrive late and include only notified cases.
- **Weather at one point per district.** A district centre does not represent a whole district.
- **Unusual periods.** The 2017 epidemic and the 2020–21 disruption.
- **What weather cannot explain.** Serotype changes, immunity, movement, control campaigns.
- **Few outbreaks in the test period.** Alert metrics rest on a small number of events.
- **Trees cannot predict beyond what they have seen.**
- **Decision support only.** Alerts support expert judgement and do not replace it.

Stating limits does not weaken a report. It tells the reader you understand your own work, and it is the first thing an examiner looks for.

## 7. Style rules
- **One idea per paragraph.** Its first sentence says what the paragraph is about.
- **Short sentences, common words.** "Use", not "utilise".
- **Define every term** at first use: horizon, endemic channel, lead time.
- **Past tense** for what you did; **present tense** for what the figures show and for general facts.
- **Every figure and table** has a number, a caption that states its message, and is mentioned in the text.
- **Units, always.** Cases per week, millimetres, weeks.
- **Cite** every idea, dataset and figure that is not yours.
- **Be exact about uncertainty.** "Suggests" and "is associated with", not "proves" and "causes".

## 8. The README
A README is the report in miniature, for a reader with two minutes:
1. One sentence: what it is.
2. A screenshot of the dashboard.
3. The headline result, with its comparison.
4. The architecture diagram: [[09.02 Architecture Diagrams]].
5. How to run it locally, as commands that can be copied.
6. The API endpoints with examples.
7. Limitations.
8. Data sources and credits.

## 9. Common mistakes
- **Writing in the order things happened** ("first I tried…") instead of the order a reader needs.
- **A methods section nobody could repeat.**
- **Results mixed with opinion.**
- **A number with no baseline.**
- **Showing only successes.**
- **No limitations, or a single vague line.**
- **"Proves" where "suggests" is all the evidence supports.** Especially: correlation presented as cause.
- **Figures with no caption or no axis labels.**
- **Copying text.** Rewrite in your own words and cite. If you use AI assistance in writing, follow your university's rules on declaring it.
- **Leaving the writing to the last weekend.** Keep a results table and notes from Phase 4 onwards.

## 10. Exam and viva focus
- **IMRaD** and what each part contains.
- **Abstract vs introduction vs conclusion.**
- **Why must methods be reproducible?**
- **What is plagiarism, and how is it avoided?** Citing sources; quoting or paraphrasing properly.
- **Why state limitations?** Honesty, and so readers know where the results apply.
- **Results vs discussion:** what was found vs what it means.
- **Qualities of good technical writing:** clear, concise, accurate, organised for the reader.
