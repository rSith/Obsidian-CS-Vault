# 09.04 · Responsible AI in Health

> [!info] [[09.00 Documentation and Portfolio|Lesson 9]] · Roadmap Phase 9 · Priority 🔴 Core
> **Builds on:** [[14.05 Ethical Considerations and Limitations of AI]] · [[10.09 E-Commerce Ethics and Ethical Principles]] · [[10.10 GDPR]]
> **Used for:** the ethics section of the report, the wording on the dashboard, and how you describe the project to others.

## 1. Definition
> [!note] Responsible AI
> **Responsible AI** means designing, building and using AI systems so that they are safe, fair, transparent and accountable, and so that people remain in control of decisions that affect them.

## 2. Why it matters here
A wrong film recommendation wastes an evening. A wrong health warning can cost more:

| Mistake | Possible harm |
|---|---|
| **Missed outbreak** (false negative) | No preparation; illness that could have been prevented |
| **False alarm** (false positive) | Staff, money and insecticide spent where they were not needed; less for a district that needed them |
| **Many false alarms** | People stop believing the warnings, including the true ones |
| **Overconfident display** | An official treats a rough estimate as a certainty |

The system does not need to be deployed for this to matter. A public dashboard with a map of red districts can be read and shared by anyone.

## 3. How to think about it: WHO's six principles
The World Health Organization's guidance on the ethics and governance of AI for health (2021) sets out six principles.

| # | Principle | Applied to this project |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Protect human autonomy** | People make the decisions. The dashboard says "decision-support tool", and an alert is advice. |
| 2 | **Promote human well-being, safety and the public interest** | Measure and report missed outbreaks and false alarms. Do not release what you have not tested. |
| 3 | **Ensure transparency, explainability and intelligibility** | Publish the method, data sources and limits. Explain alerts with [[04.07 SHAP and Feature Importance]]. Use plain words on the dashboard. |
| 4 | **Foster responsibility and accountability** | State who built it, how to report a problem, and which model version made each forecast. |
| 5 | **Ensure inclusiveness and equity** | Check performance for **every** district, not only the large ones. Make the dashboard accessible. |
| 6 | **Promote AI that is responsive and sustainable** | Monitor after release and retrain or withdraw when it degrades: [[08.05 Model Monitoring - Data Drift and Concept Drift]]. |

## 4. Risks specific to this project
### Bias from the data
The model learns from **notified** cases. A district with fewer clinics, or slower reporting, shows fewer cases than it really has. The model then learns that this district is "low risk", and the endemic channel sets a low "normal". The districts with the weakest health systems can end up looking safest.

> [!warning] The data measures reporting, not only disease
> State this in the limitations. Where you can, compare error and alert performance across districts and say which are least reliable.

### Privacy
- The project uses **counts per district per week**. No names, addresses or records of individual patients.
- Aggregated counts carry little privacy risk. If you ever work with smaller areas or individual records, that changes completely, and permission and ethical approval are needed.
- Very small counts in a small area can still point to individuals. District level avoids this.

### Data permission
Reading a public report is not the same as having the right to republish its data. Keep raw case files out of the public repository, credit the source, and ask if you are unsure.

### Misuse and misreading
- A screenshot of the map can travel without its caveats. Put "not an official forecast", the date and the source **on the page itself**, where a screenshot will include them.
- Do not name the tool or describe it in a way that suggests it comes from a health authority.

## 5. Example: an ethics section for the report
> **Ethical considerations.** This system is a decision-support tool and does not replace the judgement of public-health professionals or official surveillance. It uses only aggregated, publicly reported case counts and contains no data about individuals. Reported counts reflect notification practices as well as disease, so districts with weaker reporting may appear at lower risk than they are; performance is reported separately for each district in Table [n]. On unseen data the system missed [K] of [M] outbreaks and raised [F] false alarms; these are analysed in Section [n]. Forecasts are shown with uncertainty ranges, the date of the underlying data and the model version. The system is not validated for operational use, and any such use would require evaluation with the responsible health authority.

Six sentences cover: the role of the tool, the data, the bias, the errors, transparency, and the limit of validation.

## 6. A short checklist
- [ ] The dashboard says what the tool is and is not
- [ ] Uncertainty is shown, in words as well as a band
- [ ] The date of the data and the model version are visible
- [ ] Missed outbreaks and false alarms are counted and reported
- [ ] Performance is reported for every district
- [ ] Data sources are credited; raw case data is not republished without permission
- [ ] The report has an ethics section and a limitations section
- [ ] A domain expert has been asked for feedback

## 7. Common mistakes
- **"It is only a student project."** A public page is public.
- **Reporting only overall accuracy,** which hides the districts where it fails.
- **Presenting forecasts as facts,** with no range.
- **Words like "predicts outbreaks" or "detects dengue".** It estimates risk from weather and past counts.
- **Assuming the data is neutral.**
- **Ethics as a paragraph added the night before.** These questions shape the thresholds, the dashboard wording and the evaluation.
- **Never talking to anyone who works in public health.** The roadmap's last step, asking a domain expert, is the most valuable one.

## 8. Exam and viva focus
- **Name principles of ethical AI:** fairness, transparency and explainability, accountability, privacy, safety, human oversight.
- **WHO's six principles** for AI in health.
- **What is algorithmic bias, and where does it come from?** Unrepresentative or skewed data, design choices, and the way results are used.
- **Why does explainability matter in health?** Decisions affect people and must be justified.
- **Human-in-the-loop:** a person reviews or makes the final decision.
- **False positives vs false negatives:** which is worse depends on the cost of each.
- **Anonymised and aggregated data:** why it reduces privacy risk, and when it is not enough.
- **Decision support vs decision making.**
